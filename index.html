Waveguides

A photonic waveguide guides light by surrounding a high-index core with lower-index cladding — in silicon photonics, a silicon core (n ≈ 3.48) sitting on buried oxide (n ≈ 1.44). Total internal reflection traps the light in the core, but the cross-section decides how it travels. Above a certain width the guide supports higher-order modes, which interfere with the fundamental mode and corrupt whatever signal you are carrying. Below it, only the fundamental mode survives — that is the single-mode regime almost every silicon photonic link is designed to stay in. Push the width too far down and the mode spreads out of the core into the cladding, where it is lost to scattering and substrate leakage. Single-mode design is choosing the window between those two failures.

Single-mode cutoff

  • Sweeping waveguide width to find where higher-order modes cut off
  • Comparing multimode and single-mode behavior in the same geometry
  • Simulating the higher-order modes directly rather than inferring them

Bend loss

  • Sweeping width and bend radius to map where a bend starts radiating

A bent waveguide loses light because the outer edge of the mode would have to outrun the speed of light in the cladding to keep up with the inner edge; instead it radiates away. Loss climbs sharply below a critical radius, and wider guides tolerate tighter bends — but only up to the point where they stop being single-mode. Bend radius is therefore not a layout detail; it is the constraint that sets how small a photonic circuit can be.

Ring resonators

A ring resonator is a closed loop of waveguide placed close enough to a straight bus waveguide for light to couple across the gap. Wavelengths whose round trip comes back in phase build up inside the ring and disappear from the through port; everything else passes by untouched. That makes a ring a wavelength-selective filter you can build out of nothing but a bend. Its free spectral range is set by the round-trip length, its Q by how little light is lost per lap, and its resonance shifts with temperature or an applied voltage — which is what turns the same structure into a modulator, a multiplexer, or a sensor depending on what you attach to it.

Where this is going

The cutoff and bend-loss sweeps above are not separate exercises — they are the two numbers a ring design starts from. The ring has to stay single-mode, and its radius sets both its free spectral range and how much light it loses every lap, so the useful design space is the overlap of those two studies. Having worked on the packaging and validation side of optical I/O at Ayar Labs and Silitronics, I want to move upstream into the device itself: integrated photonics research on ring resonators, building toward resonant filters and modulators for optical links.
