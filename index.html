INVERSE_DESIGN.md

Photonic design usually works forward. You pick a geometry (a waveguide width, a grating pitch, a ring radius), simulate it, adjust it, and simulate again until the numbers are good enough. Inverse design runs the other direction. You specify the optical behavior you want and let an optimizer look for a geometry that produces it. Adjoint methods are what make that practical: one extra simulation tells you how every point in the design affects the output, so gradient descent can do the searching. The shapes that come out tend to be smaller and stranger than anything a person would draw, and they often beat hand-tuned designs on bandwidth, footprint, or loss.

AI is what lets this run at scale. A neural network trained as a surrogate for an electromagnetic solver replaces hours of FDTD with a single forward pass, which is the difference between checking hundreds of candidate geometries and checking millions. Automatic differentiation does the rest: it lets you differentiate through a physical simulation and get a gradient telling you which way to move the geometry.

This is the work I want to do. Device design cycles that take months of manual iteration should take hours.

LUMERICAL.exe

Ansys Lumerical · waveguide and resonator simulation — ongoing

Waveguides

A waveguide guides light by putting a high-index core inside lower-index cladding. In silicon photonics that is a silicon core (n ≈ 3.48) on buried oxide (n ≈ 1.44). Total internal reflection keeps the light in the core, and the cross-section decides how it travels. Above a certain width the guide supports higher-order modes, which interfere with the fundamental mode and corrupt the signal. Below that width only the fundamental mode survives, which is the single-mode regime nearly every silicon photonic link stays in. Go too narrow and the mode spreads out into the cladding, where it scatters and leaks into the substrate. The useful window sits between those two failures, and mapping it is what I am doing now.

Single-mode cutoff, in progress

  • Sweeping waveguide width to find where higher-order modes cut off
  • Comparing multimode and single-mode behavior in the same geometry
  • Simulating the higher-order modes directly rather than inferring them

Bend loss, in progress

  • Sweeping width and bend radius to map where a bend starts radiating

A bend radiates because the outer edge of the mode would have to outrun the speed of light in the cladding to keep up with the inner edge. Loss climbs sharply below a critical radius. Wider guides tolerate tighter bends, but only until they stop being single-mode. These sweeps are still running. Bend radius is what sets how small a photonic circuit can get, so I would rather measure it than guess.

Ring resonators, next

A ring resonator is a closed loop of waveguide placed close enough to a straight bus waveguide that light couples across the gap. Wavelengths whose round trip comes back in phase build up inside the ring and drop out of the through port. Everything else passes. Round-trip length sets the free spectral range, loss per lap sets the Q, and the resonance moves with temperature or an applied voltage, which is how the same structure becomes a filter, a modulator, or a sensor depending on what you attach to it. I haven't simulated any rings yet. This is where the waveguide work is headed.

Where this is going

The cutoff and bend-loss sweeps are the two numbers a ring design starts from. The ring has to stay single-mode, and its radius fixes both the free spectral range and the loss per lap, so the design space is the overlap of those two studies. I've worked the packaging and validation side of optical I/O at Ayar Labs and Silitronics. I want to move upstream to the device itself, toward resonant filters and modulators for optical links.
