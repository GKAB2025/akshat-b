INVERSE_DESIGN.md

Photonic design has traditionally worked forward: an engineer picks a geometry — a waveguide width, a grating pitch, a ring radius — and simulates it, adjusts it, and simulates it again until the performance is good enough. Inverse design flips that process. Instead of guessing a shape and checking what it does, you specify the optical behavior you want and let an optimizer — often gradient-based, using adjoint methods to compute how every point in the design affects the output in a single simulation — search the space of possible geometries for one that produces it. The results are frequently stranger and smaller than anything a person would have drawn by hand, and they routinely outperform hand-tuned designs on bandwidth, footprint, or loss.

AI is what turns inverse design from a slow, one-device-at-a-time optimization into something closer to a design system. Neural networks trained as fast surrogates for electromagnetic simulation can replace hours of finite-difference time-domain solving with a forward pass, which is what makes it possible to search millions of candidate geometries instead of hundreds. The same machinery that makes large language models trainable — automatic differentiation, gradient descent at scale — is what makes it possible to differentiate all the way through a physical simulation and ask "which way do I nudge this geometry to get closer to what I want."

I want to work at that intersection — bridging AI and photonics — to collapse device design cycles that used to take months of manual iteration down to hours, by replacing hand-tuned geometry with adjoint- and AI-accelerated optimization.

LUMERICAL.exe

Ansys Lumerical · waveguide and resonator simulation — ongoing

Waveguides

I'm currently working through waveguide design: a photonic waveguide guides light by surrounding a high-index core with lower-index cladding — in silicon photonics, a silicon core (n ≈ 3.48) sitting on buried oxide (n ≈ 1.44). Total internal reflection traps the light in the core, but the cross-section decides how it travels. Above a certain width the guide supports higher-order modes, which interfere with the fundamental mode and corrupt whatever signal you are carrying. Below it, only the fundamental mode survives — that is the single-mode regime almost every silicon photonic link is designed to stay in. Push the width too far down and the mode spreads out of the core into the cladding, where it is lost to scattering and substrate leakage. Right now I'm mapping out that window between the two failure modes.

Single-mode cutoff — in progress

  • Sweeping waveguide width to find where higher-order modes cut off
  • Comparing multimode and single-mode behavior in the same geometry
  • Simulating the higher-order modes directly rather than inferring them

Bend loss — in progress

  • Sweeping width and bend radius to map where a bend starts radiating

A bent waveguide loses light because the outer edge of the mode would have to outrun the speed of light in the cladding to keep up with the inner edge; instead it radiates away. Loss climbs sharply below a critical radius, and wider guides tolerate tighter bends — but only up to the point where they stop being single-mode. I'm still running these sweeps, since bend radius isn't just a layout detail; it's the constraint that sets how small a photonic circuit can be.

Ring resonators — next up

A ring resonator is a closed loop of waveguide placed close enough to a straight bus waveguide for light to couple across the gap. Wavelengths whose round trip comes back in phase build up inside the ring and disappear from the through port; everything else passes by untouched. That makes a ring a wavelength-selective filter you can build out of nothing but a bend. Its free spectral range is set by the round-trip length, its Q by how little light is lost per lap, and its resonance shifts with temperature or an applied voltage — which is what turns the same structure into a modulator, a multiplexer, or a sensor depending on what you attach to it. I haven't started simulating rings yet, but this is where the waveguide work is headed.

Where this is going

The cutoff and bend-loss sweeps I'm running now aren't separate exercises — they're the two numbers a ring design will start from. The ring has to stay single-mode, and its radius sets both its free spectral range and how much light it loses every lap, so the useful design space is the overlap of those two studies, once I get there. Having worked on the packaging and validation side of optical I/O at Ayar Labs and Silitronics, I'm trying to move upstream into the device itself: integrated photonics research on ring resonators, building toward resonant filters and modulators for optical links.
